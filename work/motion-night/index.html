4–5 SEPTEMBER 2026 · ONE SESSION

Motion,
measured.

Seventeen short films made in one night, in the order they were made. They are not a portfolio — they are a record of learning something the hard way, and several of them are here precisely because they failed.

The measuring found what the eye could not: every video made without real screen-recording footage was frozen, up to 93% of frames identical to the one before. The footage had been doing the moving; the animation never was. Then the measuring itself turned out to be the problem — the film with the most genuine craft scored worst of everything, and a film containing no animation at all scored the highest number recorded.

LINES

6.7s · BUILT TO THE SHAPE OF 10

Aufan pulled films 11–26 and said “make a new one like index 10.” So this is LEARNED's recipe exactly — one object through four states with no cut, a cursor as protagonist, hand-drawn smears on the fast moves, squash and stretch, a ground that performs, and a specular rake at 9px a frame because sub-pixel motion is not motion. New subject, and his own palette rather than LEARNED's: putty shell, violet-charcoal room, signal rose.

Everything on screen was measured off his own repo. The studio site is 76.1 KB of code with no package.json, no build step and no framework. Its app.js is 487 lines across fourteen functions, and the card opens into a real map of them — one thin row per real line of source, each column as tall as that function is long. The tallest is goTo() at 59 lines, and goTo is the mechanic: the clunk, the lamp dying, the tray turning one detent, the slide seating. So the card folds into the carousel tray itself, turns, and opens on the number.

Measured against the film it was built from: LEARNED 65.7% frozen, LINES 64.6% — the same register on purpose. Both “fail” the frozen gate, which is the point: that gate is calibrated on reels carrying real footage, and the film he actually kept fails it too. LINES also passes the contrast and palette checks that LEARNED was never put through.

EMULSION

6.7s · FIRST REAL 3D

The first film here with real lighting in it. Not a gradient pretending to be light — an actual studio light source, and you can see it reflected in the plastic as a rose streak down the mount's edge. That reflection is the difference between an object that looks photographed and one that looks drawn, and no CSS build on this page has it.

The object is a mounted 35mm slide, at real proportions — a 50mm square with a 36×24mm window. It is chosen out of a carrier, lifted, turned edge-on through the key light, and the instant the light passes through it the emulsion appears. That is literally how you look at a slide, and it means the light is what shows you the work — no caption has to say “here is a project”. Then it seats, on the sound his own site plays.

Four words on screen. One object. No cut. And unlike the two films it was built from, it passes the frozen-frame gate outright — 42.9% against LEARNED's 65.7% and LINES' 64.6% — because a real camera orbiting a real lit object changes every pixel of every frame, which is a thing flat graphics cannot do however hard they are animated.

Getting here cost one config file the project had never had (without it every 3D frame renders black) and one verified dead end: the standard depth-of-field and glow library does not work in this renderer, and worse, it fails silently — the picture renders and the effects are simply absent, byte-for-byte identical to not using it.

Ten films, everything after them gone, and one built back from the survivor. Films 11–26 were pulled on 5 September — Aufan looked at the run and said so plainly. They are not archived, hidden or waiting: the numbers are retired and never reused. What is left is what he actually kept.

What the ten are evidence of. One: choosing the right object beats fixing the film later. Two: a measurement is not a verdict — LEARNED has the most real craft here and every gate ranked it last, at 86% frozen. Three: a written rule changes nothing; only a tool that refuses to let the work continue ever changed anything.

And the hardest one, which took sixteen deletions to learn: a full green board is not a film worth watching.
