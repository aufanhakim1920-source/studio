Snow that fell before there were people.

Kirkwood holds 41 kilometres of polar ice, cut into 2,412 cores and kept at fifty-five below. The deepest of them reached 3,270 metres and came up carrying air that last touched the sky 1.2 million years ago. Drag down the core to go back.

Snow lands, buries, and turns to ice. Every year leaves a band. Deeper down the weight above squeezes those bands together and forces the trapped air into the crystal, so the layers tighten and the bubbles disappear.

07 / QUESTIONS FOUR ENTRIES
01Who can request a core?

Access is by research request. Applications come from institutions, and are assessed on whether the question needs this ice specifically — because once a section is cut, it is gone. Expect a six-week lead time from submission to shipment.

02Why is the store kept at −55 °C?

Warmer ice lets trapped air move between layers, and a core that has mixed is a core that can no longer be dated. Fifty-five below is the margin that keeps 1.2 million years of atmosphere sitting exactly where it fell.

03How is 41 km of core stored?

In one-metre sections, sleeved and racked by depth rather than by site. The 2,412 cores in the collection run to 41 kilometres end to end — roughly the length of the Derwent, laid out on shelves in Hobart.

04What does the deepest core hold?

The 3,270 m core reaches ice that formed 1.2 million years ago. At that depth the annual layers are millimetres apart and the bubbles have vanished into the crystal, so the air is read by melting rather than by crushing.
