No. 41 · Infrastructure

The day with
two noons

For most of human history every town kept its own time, and nobody found that confusing. It took the railways — and one very long Sunday in 1883 — to make a continent agree on what o'clock it was.

By H. Okonkwo 18 November —

Before the trains, noon was an observation rather than an agreement. It was the moment the sun stood highest over your particular chimney, and it arrived in Bristol about ten minutes after it arrived in London. Nobody minded. A ten-minute disagreement between two towns is only a problem if something has to leave one and arrive at the other on a schedule.

Then something did. By 1840 the Great Western Railway was running trains fast enough that the difference between local times became a matter of collision rather than pedantry, and it did the obvious thing: it printed its timetables in London time and told every station along the line to keep it.

The compromise was visible on the clock faces. Several stations simply fitted a second minute hand, painted a different colour, so that a passenger could read both the time the train would leave and the time the town believed it was. It is a very British solution: rather than settle the argument, mount it on the wall.

The problem crosses an ocean

Britain is small enough that one time could be imposed by a railway company and absorbed by everybody else within a decade. North America was not. By the early 1880s the continent's railways were running on fifty-three different local times, and a traveller from Maine to California passed through more than twenty of them.

The man who fixed it was not a government. William F. Allen edited the Travelers' Official Guide, which meant he was the person who actually had to print the timetables, and he had been complaining about the arithmetic for years. In October 1883 he put a proposal to the General Time Convention: five zones, each an hour wide, each a whole number of hours from Greenwich.

“The proposition is a simple one. Let the country be divided into belts of fifteen degrees, and let all clocks within a belt agree.”

W. F. Allen, to the General Time Convention, October 1883

It passed. The date chosen was Sunday 18 November 1883, at noon — a Sunday because that was the quietest day on the network, and noon because that was when the correction would be smallest.

The day itself

In cities east of a zone boundary the clocks went back. Noon arrived, was acknowledged, and then arrived again some minutes later once the telegraph signal came through from the Naval Observatory. Newspapers called it the day with two noons, and the phrase stuck.

Not everyone accepted it. Bangor, Maine kept its own time until 1887. Detroit held out until 1900, arguing — not unreasonably — that a city should not take its hour from a railway company. Cincinnati's council resolved that the sun, and not a corporation, would say when it was noon; the resolution lasted about eight months.

What is striking, reading the objections now, is that almost none of them are about accuracy. They are about jurisdiction. The complaint was never that railway time was wrong — it was that nobody had asked. Standard time in America was not legislated until 1918, thirty-five years after everybody had already started using it.

What it cost

The bargain was this: you gave up a time that was true of your particular place, and you got a time that was true of everybody's schedule. Solar noon in Detroit now falls at about 12:33. The clock is wrong about the sun, permanently and by design, and that is the price of the trains being right about each other.

It is worth remembering the shape of that trade the next time a standard is imposed on something that used to be local. The argument is rarely that the standard is better. It is that coordination is worth more than fit — which is true, right up until it isn't.
