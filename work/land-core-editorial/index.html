I·The Passage

What a metre is worth

Two thousand four hundred and twelve cores are held at Kirkwood, and every one of them is a column of weather standing on end. Near the top the snow is barely pressed — a single year makes a band you can see from across the room, and the air inside is still air, sealed in bubbles that can be opened and breathed out into an instrument. Push down and the years get thinner. By a thousand metres a year is a hair. By three thousand it is closer to a rumour: a stripe the width of a pencil line holding a decade, then a century.

Pressure takes the bubbles first. Somewhere below eight hundred metres the ice stops keeping air in pockets and starts keeping it inside its own crystal structure, and the core comes up clear as window glass. That is the passage this page is drawn from. Drag the ice and you are moving through it — the bands close up, the light stops travelling, and the figure at the foot of the page counts backwards past every summer nobody was there to see.

The deepest metre we hold is not the oldest thing in the building.
It is only the quietest.

Kirkwood began in 1987 in a shed behind the Hobart wharf, with one freezer and nine cores borrowed from a returning voyage. It now runs 41 km of shelf at −55 °C, cold enough that the building’s own steel is inspected for it. Nothing here is on display. The cores are lent, read and returned, and the archive is measured not in visitors but in the papers that come back.

II·Access

How the ice is lent

Kirkwood does not sell ice and does not open to the public. Cores are released against a research request, reviewed by the collections committee, and cut to the shortest length the question needs. Allow a six-week lead time between the request and the courier — the cold chain is the slow part, not the paperwork.

Who may request a core?

Any researcher attached to an institution with cold-chain handling. We also release to teaching collections, in sections under 200 mm.

How long does a request take?

Six weeks from a complete submission. Four of those are freight and customs; the committee sits fortnightly.

Can I visit the shelves?

No. The store is held at −55 °C and entry is limited to two staff at a time, in twenty-minute shifts.

What happens to a core after it is read?

It comes back. Every borrowed section is returned to its own place on the shelf, with the cut logged against the original 3,270 m depth record.
